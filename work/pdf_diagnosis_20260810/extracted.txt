Project Management ­ Module 4 Notes

Overview

These notes cover the fourth and final module of Project Management: reducing project
duration through crashing, project risk management, project evaluation, project progress and
performance management, project closure and oversight, and an introduction to project
management software. The notes are written for exam preparation with clear definitions,
formulas, comparison tables, and likely exam questions.

1. Reducing Project Duration

Reducing project duration means shortening the total time required to complete a project. This
becomes necessary due to client demands, contractual penalties, competitive pressure,
strategic urgency, or schedule overruns.[web:51][web:50]

Reasons for reducing project duration

    Market or competitive pressure to launch earlier.[web:51]
    Contractual deadlines with penalty clauses.[web:52]
    Reallocation of resources to another project.[web:51]
    Schedule recovery after delays.[web:50]
    External factors such as regulatory or seasonal deadlines.[web:51]

Options for reducing project duration

    Adding more resources to critical activities.[web:52]
    Outsourcing project activities to specialized parties.[web:52]
    Working overtime or extending working shifts.[web:52]
    Reducing project scope after consulting stakeholders.[web:52]
    Phasing the project deliverables so parts are released earlier.[web:52]
    Fast-tracking: overlapping phases or activities that were originally sequential.[web:63]

2. Crashing Project Activities

Project crashing is the process of shortening the project schedule duration for the least
additional cost by adding extra resources to critical path activities.[web:50][web:53]
Key concepts

    Normal time: The standard duration estimated under normal conditions.[web:52]
    Crash time: The minimum possible time to complete an activity after applying extra
    resources.[web:52]
    Normal cost: The cost of completing an activity in its normal time.[web:52]
    Crash cost: The higher cost incurred when completing the activity in crash time.[web:52]
    Crash cost per unit time: The additional cost for reducing one unit of duration.[web:53]

Crash cost per unit time formula

The activity with the smallest crash cost per unit of time on the critical path should be crashed
first.[web:53]

Steps in crashing a project

  1. Start with all activities at normal time and normal cost.[web:52]
  2. Find the critical path and total project duration.[web:52]
  3. Calculate crash cost per unit of time for each critical activity.[web:53]
  4. Select the critical activity with the lowest crash cost per unit.[web:53]
  5. Crash that activity by one time unit and recalculate total cost and duration.[web:53]
  6. Update the critical path (it may change after crashing).[web:53]
  7. Repeat until target duration is reached or further crashing is not worthwhile.[web:50]

    [web:53]

Important rules for crashing

    Only activities on the critical path can reduce overall project duration.[web:52][web:53]
    If there are multiple critical paths, one activity from each must be crashed simultaneously.
    [web:53]
    Crashing beyond the crash time is not possible.[web:52]
    Total project cost = direct cost + indirect cost; reduce total cost by finding the optimal crash
    point.[web:52]

Crashing vs Fast-Tracking

 Basis                  Crashing                              Fast-Tracking
Method  Add more resources [web:50][web:63]  Overlap sequential activities [web:63]
Cost    Increases cost [web:52][web:53]      May increase risk but not necessarily cost [web:63]
 Basis                  Crashing                                         Fast-Tracking
Risk    Lower risk if resources are available [web:63]  Higher risk due to parallel work [web:63]
Scope   Scope unchanged [web:52][web:63]                Scope unchanged [web:63]

3. Project Risk Management

Project risk management is the systematic process of identifying, analyzing, planning
responses to, and controlling risks in order to protect project objectives from uncertainty.
[web:54][web:59]

What is risk?

Risk is an uncertain event or condition that, if it occurs, has a positive or negative effect on
project objectives including scope, schedule, cost, and quality. Risk can be a threat (negative) or
an opportunity (positive).[web:54]

Steps in project risk management

Step 1: Risk Identification

    Identify all potential risks that might affect the project.[web:54]
    Tools: brainstorming, checklists, expert interviews, SWOT analysis, historical records.
    [web:54][web:59]
    Output: Risk Register -- a document listing all identified risks.[web:54]

Step 2: Risk Analysis

Qualitative risk analysis: Prioritize risks using probability and impact rating. Each risk is
assessed for its likelihood and consequence on a scale (e.g., Low, Medium, High).[web:54]
[web:59]

Quantitative risk analysis: Use numerical tools to estimate the probability and financial impact
of risks. Tools include Monte Carlo simulation, decision tree analysis, and sensitivity analysis.
[web:54]

Step 3: Risk Response Planning

Develop strategies to reduce threats and enhance opportunities:[web:54][web:59]

    Strategy        For Threats                           Meaning
Avoid [web:54]     Yes           Change the plan to eliminate the risk entirely
Transfer [web:54]  Yes           Shift the financial impact to a third party (insurance, contracts)
Mitigate [web:54]  Yes           Reduce probability or impact
    Strategy       For Threats                            Meaning
Accept [web:54]   Yes            Acknowledge the risk and prepare a contingency
Exploit [web:54]  Opportunities  Take advantage of positive risk

Step 4: Risk Monitoring and Control

    Track identified risks and identify new ones throughout the project.[web:54]
    Conduct risk audits and review risk register periodically.[web:54]
    Implement contingency plans when risks occur.[web:54]

4. Project Evaluation

Project evaluation is the systematic assessment of project objectives, processes, performance,
and outcomes. It helps determine whether a project is achieving its intended goals and
provides a basis for decision-making and improvement.[web:55][web:60]

Types of evaluation

    Pre-project evaluation: Assesses viability before the project starts.
    Mid-project evaluation: Checks whether the project is on track during execution.
    Post-project evaluation: Reviews outcomes after closure to assess impact and lessons.

Criteria for evaluation

    Achievement of scope and deliverables.
    Adherence to schedule and cost baselines.
    Quality of outputs and stakeholder satisfaction.[web:60]
    Efficiency of resource use.
    Risk management effectiveness.[web:60]

5. Project Progress and Performance Management

Project progress monitoring

Progress monitoring tracks the actual status of work against the planned baseline. It answers
questions like: Is the project on schedule? Is spending on track? Are deliverables being
produced correctly?[web:60]
Earned Value Management (EVM)

EVM is the most widely used technique for measuring project performance. It integrates scope,
time, and cost to provide an objective view of project health.[web:55][web:60]

Interpretation

    CV > 0: Under budget; CV < 0: Over budget.[web:60]
    SV > 0: Ahead of schedule; SV < 0: Behind schedule.[web:60]

Performance reporting

    Regular performance reports compare actual work, cost, and time against the plan.
    Reports help stakeholders stay informed and enable timely corrective action.[web:60]

6. Project Closure and Oversight

Project closure is the formal final phase in which all project activities are completed,
deliverables are handed over, contracts are closed, resources are released, and project records
are finalized and archived.[web:56][web:61]

Why project closure is important

    Ensures all deliverables are formally accepted by the client.[web:56]
    Releases resources for other projects.[web:61]
    Documents lessons learned for future projects.[web:56][web:61]
    Reduces organizational risk from unresolved contracts or commitments.[web:56]
    Provides a complete and auditable record of the project.[web:61]

Steps in project closure

  1. Verify scope: Confirm all deliverables have been completed and accepted.[web:61]
  2. Transfer deliverables: Hand over project outputs to the client or operations team.[web:61]
  3. Close contracts: Formally close all supplier and subcontractor agreements.[web:61]
  4. Release resources: Reassign project team members, equipment, and materials.[web:61]
  5. Document lessons learned: Record what went well, what went wrong, and how to improve.

    [web:56][web:61]
  6. Prepare close-out report: Summarize project performance including cost, schedule,

    quality, and risk outcomes.[web:61]
  7. Archive project records: Store documentation for future reference and audit.[web:56]

    [web:61]
  8. Celebrate and formally close: Hold a close-out meeting and officially announce project

    completion.[web:61]

Close-out report contents

    Summary of project objectives and deliverables.[web:61]
    Performance data on schedule, cost, and quality.[web:61]
    Description of risks encountered and how they were handled.[web:61]
    Lessons learned documentation.[web:61]
    Final recommendations for related future projects.[web:61]

Project oversight

Project oversight means the ongoing review and governance of a project to ensure it remains
aligned with organizational goals, complies with policies, and delivers its intended value.
Oversight mechanisms include:

    Steering committee reviews.
    Independent project audits.
    Phase gate reviews before proceeding to the next stage.
    Portfolio-level monitoring of multiple projects.
7. Introduction to Project Management Software

Project management software provides digital tools that support planning, scheduling,
resource management, communication, collaboration, cost tracking, and reporting for
projects.[web:57][web:62]

Why use project management software?

    Automates scheduling and network diagram creation.[web:57]
    Centralizes information and improves team collaboration.[web:62]
    Tracks costs, time, and performance in real time.[web:57]
    Generates Gantt charts, PERT/CPM charts, and progress reports automatically.[web:62]
    Manages resource allocation and workload balancing.[web:57]

Categories of project management software

    Category                         Description                                       Example tools
Desktop software   Installed on a local system, powerful for scheduling  MS Project [web:62]
Cloud-based tools  Accessible online, supports team collaboration        Asana, Trello, monday.com [web:57][web:62]
Enterprise tools   Full-featured, integrated with ERP/finance systems    Oracle Primavera, Jira [web:62]
Open-source tools  Free and customizable                                 GanttProject, OpenProject [web:62]
